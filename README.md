# Animal cognition messaging shifts humans’ emotions but not behavior: A randomized experiment across chimpanzees and ʻalalā


This repository contains the code for the analyses in the manuscript "Animal cognition messaging shifts humans’ emotions but not behavior: A randomized experiment across chimpanzees and ʻalalā". 

## Abstract
Conservation depends on changing human behavior. Most campaigns rely on charismatic species and threat-based appeals. An underexplored alternative is animal cognition messaging (i.e., framing species through their mental capacities). We conducted a randomized experiment (N=563) testing whether cognition-based messaging outperforms traditional conservation messaging in shifting emotions, attitudes, and pro-conservation behavior. Participants viewed a short video of one of two endangered species differing in familiarity and charisma: the chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes) or the ʻAlalā (Corvus hawaiiensis), paired with one of four randomly assigned voiceovers in a 2×2 design (cognition, conservation, combined, control). We measured affect, attitudes, petition-signing intention, and actual donation behavior. Message framing strongly shaped emotion: cognition messaging elicited the most positive affect and the lowest negative affect, whereas conservation and combined messaging increased negative affect. Cognition framing also elicited larger gains in learning and empathy for the unfamiliar ʻAlalā than the familiar chimpanzee. However, these shifts did not translate into behavior: petition choices were uniform across conditions and donations were near zero (donation rates interpreted cautiously given the primarily college-aged sample demographic). Cognition messaging is a promising tool for building engagement and empathy, particularly for overlooked species, but emotional appeals alone do not reliably drive conservation action.

## Data Availability
The data used in this study are not publicly available due to participant confidentiality requirements under the University of California, Berkeley Institutional Review Board (Committee for Protection of Human Subjects / Office for Protection of Human Subjects; protocol 2025-01-18233). De-identified data may be available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request and subject to a data use agreement.
